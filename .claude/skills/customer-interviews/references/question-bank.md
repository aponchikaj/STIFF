# Question Bank: Non-Leading Questions by Interview Type

Each entry shows the non-leading question to use and the leading version it replaces. Pull the section matching your interview type, cut to 6–8 core questions, and leave half the session for follow-ups. Universal follow-ups that work after almost any answer: "Tell me more about that." / "What happened next?" / "Who else was involved?" / "What did that cost you?" / "Why was that hard?" / "What else was going on?"

---

## Discovery Interviews (30 min)

Goal: find real problems, their frequency, and evidence of existing spend — without ever describing your idea.

### Opening the wound

| Use this | Replaces this leading version |
|---|---|
| "Walk me through the last time you [did the task]." | "Do you often struggle with [the task]?" |
| "What's the hardest part of [domain area] for you right now?" | "Is [my problem hypothesis] a big problem for you?" |
| "When did you last hit that problem? Tell me the whole story." | "How often does this problem happen?" |
| "What were you trying to get done when that happened?" | "Were you trying to [my assumed goal]?" |

### Following the pain

| Use this | Replaces this leading version |
|---|---|
| "What did you do about it?" | "Would a tool that fixed this help you?" |
| "What have you tried so far? How did each attempt go?" | "Have you tried [category of solution like mine]?" |
| "Why haven't you solved this already? What's stopped you?" | "You'd solve this if a good option existed, right?" |
| "What happens if you just… don't fix it?" | "This must be really painful to leave unsolved, no?" |

If the answer to "what did you do about it?" is "nothing" — no search, no workaround, no complaint to anyone — mark the pain as low-intensity regardless of how vividly they described it.

### Digging for spend

| Use this | Replaces this leading version |
|---|---|
| "What are you paying for today that touches this problem?" | "Would you pay for a solution?" |
| "How much time did the workaround take last week?" | "Would this save you a lot of time?" |
| "Who built that spreadsheet/script? How long do they spend maintaining it?" | "Wouldn't it be great to retire that spreadsheet?" |
| "Whose budget would a fix come out of? Who'd have to approve it?" | "You could get budget for this, right?" |

### Closing (only place your idea may appear)

| Use this | Replaces this leading version |
|---|---|
| "Who else should I talk to who deals with this?" | "Do you know others who'd want this product?" |
| "Can I follow up when we have something to show? Would you try a rough version?" | "You'd definitely sign up when we launch, yeah?" |

A concrete commitment — an intro, calendar time for a pilot, a prepay — is evidence. An enthusiastic "keep me posted!" is not.

## Churn Interviews (30 min, within 2 weeks of cancel)

Goal: find the event that changed the equation, not a socially acceptable feature gap. Expect the real reason on the second or third answer.

| Use this | Replaces this leading version |
|---|---|
| "What changed around the time you decided to cancel?" | "What features were we missing?" |
| "Walk me back to when things were going well — what did a normal week with the product look like?" | "Did you stop finding the product useful?" |
| "When did you first think about leaving? What was happening that week?" | "Was it the pricing change that made you leave?" |
| "What else was going on around then?" (ask after every answer, at least twice) | "So that was the reason, then?" |
| "What did you switch to? What's better and worse about it?" | "Did you leave us for [competitor]?" |
| "Who else was involved in the decision to cancel?" | "Was this your call?" |
| "What would have had to be true for you to stay?" | "If we added [feature], would you come back?" |
| "If a colleague at another company asked whether to use us, what would you tell them?" | "You'd still recommend us though, right?" |

Note: "too expensive" as a first answer usually means "not valuable enough for the price" or "budget got cut" — keep asking "what else was going on" to find which.

## Pricing Interviews (30–45 min)

Goal: understand the value model and existing spend. Direct "would you pay $X" answers inflate ~2–3× over real behavior — never ask them. For a quantitative price point, run Van Westendorp via the pricing skill; these questions build the qualitative foundation it needs.

| Use this | Replaces this leading version |
|---|---|
| "What do you spend today — tools, people, time — on this whole workflow?" | "Would you pay $50/month for this?" |
| "Walk me through the last tool purchase you made in this category. How did you decide?" | "Is price the main factor when you buy tools like this?" |
| "What was the most recent thing you tried to buy but couldn't get approved? What happened?" | "Getting budget wouldn't be a problem for you, right?" |
| "When you renewed [current tool], what almost made you not renew?" | "You'd switch to something cheaper if it existed, wouldn't you?" |
| "What would need to break for you to go find a replacement tomorrow?" | "If we were 20% cheaper, would you switch?" |
| "How do you measure whether a tool in this category is earning its cost?" | "Would saving 5 hours a week justify $X to you?" |
| "What's the difference between the plan you're on and the one above it? Why haven't you upgraded?" | "Premium features would be worth more to you, right?" |

Anchor everything to purchases and renewals that actually happened. A story about last quarter's procurement fight is worth ten hypothetical price reactions.

## JTBD Switch Interviews (45–60 min, switchers within last 90 days)

Goal: reconstruct the timeline from first thought to switch, and surface all four forces (push, pull, anxiety, habit). Anchor to dates and concrete events; walk backwards from the purchase.

### Timeline reconstruction

| Use this | Replaces this leading version |
|---|---|
| "You signed up on [date]. What happened that day? Start from the morning." | "Why did you buy?" |
| "When did it first cross your mind that the old way wasn't working?" | "Was [assumed problem] why you started looking?" |
| "Between first thinking about it and actively looking — what were you doing? What did you notice?" | "Did you compare us against [competitor]?" |
| "What was the event that made you go from 'someday' to 'this week'?" | "Was it [my assumed trigger] that pushed you over?" |
| "Where were you when you decided? Who did you talk to?" | "Was it an easy decision?" |

### The four forces

| Force | Use this | Replaces this leading version |
|---|---|---|
| Push | "What was going on that made the old way stop being tolerable?" | "The old tool was pretty bad, right?" |
| Pull | "What did you imagine your week looking like after switching?" | "Was it [feature] that attracted you?" |
| Anxiety | "What almost stopped you? What were you worried would go wrong?" | "Any concerns? No? Great." |
| Habit | "What did you like about the old way? What did you give up by switching?" | "You don't miss the old tool, do you?" |

### After the switch

| Use this | Replaces this leading version |
|---|---|
| "First week with the new thing — what surprised you?" | "Has it been great so far?" |
| "What do you still do the old way? Why?" | "You've fully switched over, right?" |
| "If the new thing disappeared tomorrow, what would you do?" | "You couldn't live without it now, could you?" |

The triggering event you uncover is your marketing message; the anxieties are your onboarding fixes; the habits are your switching-cost problem. Also interview people who looked and did NOT switch — anxiety and habit killed the deal, and they'll tell you exactly how.
