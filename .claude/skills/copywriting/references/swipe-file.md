## How to use this file

- Fill in the pattern with the user's actual product, proof, and reader — then delete the scaffolding words that don't earn their place.
- Awareness matters: formulas 3, 7, 9, 11 target problem-aware readers; 1, 2, 5, 8, 15 target solution-aware readers; 12 and 13 work on cold traffic where you must interrupt.
- Never ship a curiosity-gap headline (13) on a surface where clarity is the job (pricing, docs, transactional email subject lines).

## The formula library

### 1. Outcome + Timeframe

**Pattern:** `[Desired outcome] in [specific timeframe]`

The timeframe does the persuading — it converts a claim into a testable promise. Use real numbers; round numbers read as made up ("in 14 days" beats "in 2 weeks" beats "fast").

- "A hiring pipeline you trust in 14 days"
- "From CSV mess to live dashboard in 4 minutes"
- "Your first paying customer in 30 days — or we work free until you get one"

### 2. Outcome Without the Pain

**Pattern:** `[Outcome] without [the thing they dread]`

Strongest when the dread is the reason they haven't acted. Name the *specific* dread, not a generic one.

- "SOC 2 compliance without hiring a consultant"
- "A second income without a second job"
- "Migrate off Heroku without a maintenance window"

### 3. The Question That Names the Pain

**Pattern:** `[Question the reader answers "yes" to, in their own words]?`

Works only if ~all of the target readers answer yes; a "no" ends the conversation. Use the reader's phrasing (from support tickets, reviews, sales calls), not marketing phrasing.

- "Still reconciling payouts in a spreadsheet?"
- "Does every deploy feel like a coin flip?"
- "Tired of writing the same onboarding email 40 times a week?"

### 4. How to X (Even If Y)

**Pattern:** `How to [outcome], even if [common disqualifier]`

The "even if" clause removes the reader's ready-made excuse. Skip the clause if there's no strong objection — plain "How to X" is fine but weaker.

- "How to run usability tests, even if you have zero users"
- "How to raise a seed round, even if you don't know a single investor"
- "How to sell out a launch, even with a 900-follower audience"

### 5. Number + Noun + Promise

**Pattern:** `[Number] [things] that [payoff]` or `[Number] ways to [outcome]`

Odd and precise numbers outperform round ones in most listicle testing ("7" and "23" beat "10"). The noun must be concrete: "mistakes", "templates", "questions" — not "insights".

- "23 onboarding emails you can steal today"
- "7 pricing-page mistakes costing you demos"
- "5 questions that kill bad feature ideas in one meeting"

### 6. Do X Like Y

**Pattern:** `[Action] like [admired expert / company the reader respects]`

Borrows credibility. Y must be someone the *reader* admires, not someone the writer admires — check with the user.

- "Write release notes like Linear"
- "Price like a category leader, not a commodity"
- "Onboard users like Duolingo onboards learners"

### 7. The Enemy Headline

**Pattern:** `[Category/tool/practice] is [broken/dead/costing you X]. [Here's the alternative.]`

Polarizing by design — it wins attention from people who share the enemy and repels the rest. Only use when the user is comfortable naming the enemy and the product genuinely replaces it.

- "Your ATS is why good candidates ghost you"
- "Timesheets are theater. Track outcomes instead."
- "Stop paying for seats nobody logs into"

### 8. Specific Proof Up Front

**Pattern:** `[Impressive verifiable number/result] — [what made it possible]` or `[Customer count/result] [outcome verb]`

Leads with evidence instead of a claim. Requires real proof; never fabricate or round up. If proof is thin, use formula 1 or 2 instead.

- "12,400 teams closed their books faster last month"
- "This checkout change added $212k/yr for a 4-person store"
- "Rated 4.9 by 2,300 agencies that switched from spreadsheets"

### 9. The "Finally" Release Valve

**Pattern:** `Finally, [thing the market has wanted but not gotten]`

The word "finally" implies a long-standing, widely felt gap — it flops if the gap isn't real. Best for genuinely novel mechanisms in tired categories.

- "Finally, analytics your PM can query without SQL"
- "Finally, a CRM that fills itself in"
- "Finally — expense reports that file themselves"

### 10. Command + Benefit

**Pattern:** `[Imperative verb] [object]. [Benefit clause.]`

Two short beats. The command creates momentum; the second beat justifies it. Great for heroes with tight space.

- "Ship on Fridays. Rollbacks take one click."
- "Delete your staging environment. Preview every PR instead."
- "Stop guessing. See which ad paid for itself."

### 11. For [Audience] Who [Struggle]

**Pattern:** `[Product/category] for [named audience] who [specific struggle or standard]`

Trades reach for resonance — the named audience clicks at much higher rates; everyone else bounces. Correct trade for niche products, wrong for broad ones.

- "Bookkeeping for freelancers who hate bookkeeping"
- "The design tool for engineers who can't draw"
- "Payroll for restaurants that tip out nightly"

### 12. The Contrarian Claim

**Pattern:** `[Widely held belief] is wrong. / You don't need [thing everyone says you need].`

An interrupt pattern for cold traffic. The body must pay off the claim immediately with the mechanism, or trust craters.

- "You don't need more traffic. You need a better first sentence."
- "Cold email isn't dead. Your first line is."
- "More features won't fix churn"

### 13. What/Why Curiosity Gap

**Pattern:** `What [respected group] knows about [topic] that you don't` / `Why [surprising fact/behavior]`

Earns clicks by withholding — which means it must never appear where clarity is the job (pricing pages, product UI, transactional subjects). Content and ads only.

- "Why our best-converting page has no images"
- "What 100 churned customers told us about onboarding"
- "The pricing question 9 out of 10 founders answer wrong"

### 14. Before/After Compression

**Pattern:** `[Painful before] → [desirable after]` (often literally with an arrow, or "From X to Y")

BAB in one line. Works when both states are instantly picturable and the contrast is large.

- "From 40-minute deploys to 4"
- "Inbox chaos → one prioritized queue"
- "From 'where's that file?' to found in 3 seconds"

### 15. The Zero-Friction Promise

**Pattern:** `[Outcome]. No [friction 1], no [friction 2], no [friction 3].`

The "no" list should name the exact frictions competitors impose. Two or three items max; four reads as protesting too much.

- "Live in production today. No sales call, no credit card, no YAML."
- "Send your first invoice free. No signup, no watermark."
- "Real-time alerts. No agents to install, no dashboards to babysit."

## Example rewrites

Each pair shows the move, not just the result. The "why" is the reusable part.

**1. Generic claim → Outcome + Timeframe (formula 1)**
- Before: "The modern platform for financial operations"
- After: "Close your books in 3 days, not 3 weeks"
- Why: "Modern platform" is unverifiable and interchangeable; the after states a measurable outcome the reader can check against their own close process. The 3/3 contrast adds a built-in before/after.

**2. Feature list → Outcome Without the Pain (formula 2)**
- Before: "Automated compliance workflows with policy templates and integrations"
- After: "Pass your SOC 2 audit without pausing product work"
- Why: The features are the *mechanism*; the reader buys the outcome. The named dread ("pausing product work") is the actual reason startups delay compliance — moved from unspoken objection to headline.

**3. We-centric → reader-centric command (formula 10)**
- Before: "We help teams collaborate more effectively across departments"
- After: "Stop losing decisions in Slack threads"
- Why: Swapped subject from "we" to the reader's pain, and replaced the abstract benefit ("collaborate effectively") with a concrete, picturable failure everyone has experienced.

**4. Clever pun → specific proof (formula 8)**
- Before: "Espresso yourself: coffee subscriptions with personality"
- After: "Freshly roasted 48 hours before it ships — rated 4.8 by 11,000 subscribers"
- Why: The pun costs a beat of comprehension and communicates nothing about quality. The rewrite spends that beat on two verifiable facts. Keep the personality for the subhead and body voice.

**5. Vague CTA → verb-first specific CTA**
- Before: "Learn more"
- After: "See a 3-minute demo" (+ under-button line: "No signup required")
- Why: "Learn more" hides the commitment level, so cautious readers don't click. Naming the format and the time cost answers "what happens when I click?" — and the microcopy line kills the remaining objection.

**6. Blaming error message → recovery-oriented microcopy**
- Before: "Invalid input. Error 422."
- After: "That date is in the past — pick today or later. (Need a past date? Use the import tool.)"
- Why: States what's wrong in plain words, gives the fix, and anticipates the legitimate edge case. The raw code moves to logs or a collapsed detail, not the headline of the moment.

**7. Empty "No data" state → activation microcopy**
- Before: "No reports found."
- After: "No reports yet. Connect a data source and your first report builds itself in about a minute. [Connect source]"
- Why: An empty state is an onboarding surface, not a status readout. The rewrite explains what belongs here, sets a time expectation, and hands the reader the next click.

**8. Buried offer → For-audience-who (formula 11)**
- Before: "A comprehensive, flexible, all-in-one solution for the modern practice"
- After: "Scheduling for dental offices that hate no-shows"
- Why: Four stacked adjectives, zero information. The rewrite names the buyer and the money problem in eight words — narrower, and that's the point: the target reader now knows this is *for them*.
